\markboth{Motivation, Objectives and Structure}{Motivation, Objectives and Structure}

In many modern workloads, moving data between separate processing and memory units consumes more energy than the arithmetic operation itself. A single off-chip memory access can cost three to four orders of magnitude more energy than an arithmetic operation~\cite{Horowitz2014}, and large machine-learning models and continuous sensor streams require frequent memory access~\cite{LeCun2015}. As gains from transistor scaling slow, data movement accounts for an increasing share of system energy and latency~\cite{Hennessy2019}. This thesis examines one physical response: devices whose internal dynamics store and transform temporal state within the same substrate~\cite{Mead1990,Tanaka2019}.

The devices studied here are \emph{volatile organic memristive devices}: two-terminal elements made from ion-containing polymer composites whose conductance retains a decaying record of recent electrical activity. Their relaxation is treated as a device function. A controlled decay makes each device a physical \emph{leaky integrator}, while devices with different decay times form a substrate for \emph{temporal} (reservoir-style) computing on time-varying signals. The thesis tests two linked propositions: composition controls the timescale in the replicated PEO/lithium-triflate series, and behavioural models fitted to the measured dynamics can perform temporal computations.

The work begins with a fully characterised Super~Yellow/Hybrane/lithium-triflate exemplar and then follows the loss of reproducibility that prompted a change to PEO. On the PEO platform, a replicated composition series tests how formulation controls the dynamics; the smaller host, anion, and cation series remain chemical hypotheses rather than material laws. Behavioural models fitted to these measurements are then evaluated on benchmark and physiological temporal-computing tasks.

\section*{Open Questions and Objectives}
\label{subsec:open_questions}

\paragraph{Realising and understanding an exemplar.} Can a fully reversible two-terminal organic memristive platform be made from a solution-processed polymer-electrolyte composite using standard organic-electronic fabrication? Can that common formulation and architecture support analogue switching, distinguishable short- and long-term memory, an excitatory post-synaptic current response, and spike-timing-dependent plasticity? Can the fast and slow memory components be explained by the composite's cation--anion mobility asymmetry?

\paragraph{Composition as the quantitative control variable.} Within a common host and a common alkali-metal triflate family, how do the device dynamics depend on composition when the polymer-to-semiconductor and salt-to-semiconductor mass ratios are varied around a reference formulation? This composition dependence is the axis the thesis sets out to \emph{replicate}. Host, anion, and cation substitutions pose hypotheses about the same dynamics, in particular whether moving the cation along the \ce{Li+}, \ce{Na+}, \ce{K+} series shifts the measured fading-memory timescale as coordination chemistry suggests.

\paragraph{Utility for temporal computing.} Can the volatile response of these devices be exploited for temporal computation? Can composition supply task-matched fading-memory timescales, while sample-limited chemistry and device-to-device spread populate a heterogeneous bank? Does measured variability broaden the computational response?

These questions motivate six objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \label{subsec:objectives} Design, fabricate, and characterise a proof-of-concept two-terminal organic memristive device based on a Super~Yellow, Hybrane, and lithium-triflate composite, processed from a single solution onto a standard ITO/composite/Ag stack, and demonstrate the basic memristive and synaptic functionality in a single platform.
  \item Provide a mechanistic account of the device's two memory regimes in terms of the displacement of mobile triflate anions and lithium cations within the oxygen-rich ion-conducting host, supported by hard--soft acid--base reasoning on the cation--oxygen interaction.
  \item Build a comparative experimental corpus on a poly(ethylene oxide)-based platform loaded with alkali-metal triflate salts, fabricated under a common protocol, and characterise it through three common dynamical measurements: I--V hysteresis, variable-number-of-pulses potentiation, and variable-delay depotentiation.
  \item Quantify the composition dependence through a replicated PEO and lithium-triflate concentration series, and survey host, anion, and cation substitutions on the same three measurements.
  \item Construct experimentally grounded behavioural models from those measurements, using composition-indexed fading-memory times, nonlinear pulse-update rules, device-to-device variability envelopes, and read-transfer behaviour.
  \item Demonstrate, in simulation, temporal-computing functionality from these models in a reservoir-computing framework: memory-capacity and NARMA benchmarks, WESAD physiological-context reconstruction, and affective-computing tasks across three corpora (WESAD stress, CASE continuous valence/arousal, and a PhysioNet Non-EEG cross-corpus replication).
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Structure of the Thesis}
\label{subsec:outline}

Chapter~1 develops the conceptual and materials background, from the limits of conventional computing to the polymer-electrolyte composite used in the experimental work. Chapter~2 presents the Super~Yellow/Hybrane/lithium-triflate proof of concept and its complete synaptic measurement suite; it is based on Prado-Socorro et al.~\cite{PradoSocorro2022}. Chapter~3 reconstructs the later loss of reproducibility, the change to a PEO host, and the fabrication-record methodology adopted for the remaining devices.

Chapter~4 tests composition on a replicated PEO/lithium-triflate grid and surveys the smaller host, anion, and cation series with the same three dynamical measurements. Chapter~5 fits behavioural models to those measurements and evaluates them on memory, system-identification, physiological-context, and affective-computing tasks. Chapter~6 compares the work with temporal and event-driven neuromorphic hardware, sets the limits of the evidence, and identifies the experiments still required.
