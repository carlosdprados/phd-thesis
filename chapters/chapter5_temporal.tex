\section{Introduction and Scope}\label{sec:ch5_intro}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

\Cref{ch:comparative} showed that composition sets the fading-memory time of these devices over about one decade of seconds. This chapter asks what that time constant can be used for. Behavioural models fitted to the measured response are tested in two roles. In the first, one composition, or a pair of compositions, is chosen for its time constant and used as a designed filter; the references are the conventional components for the same job, an $RC$ element with the same half-life and a digital filter. In the second, many devices form a reservoir: a bank of randomly masked nodes whose states feed a trained linear read-out. Banks drawn from one composition are compared with banks spanning several, and linear exponential-moving-average (EMA) banks at the same half-lives serve as controls.

Peripheral physiological signals are the test bed because their timescales overlap those of the devices without rescaling: phasic electrodermal responses, respiration, and heart-rate variability vary over seconds, and tonic levels drift over minutes. The chapter uses synthetic electrodermal signals with known responses, the Wearable Stress and Affect Detection corpus (\WESAD{}), and an independent PhysioNet corpus. Affective computing supplies the tasks and labels; the chapter does not propose a new affect classifier.

All results in this chapter are \emph{in silico}; no new devices were fabricated. Each node is a compact behavioural model parameterised by the fits in \cref{ch:comparative} (\cref{sec:ch5_model}), and every read-out is a single linear ridge regression.

\section{Measured Behavioural Model}\label{sec:ch5_model}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Each composition cell is reduced to a compact behavioural model and used as one reservoir node. The model is fitted only to the three common measurements of \cref{ch:comparative}: hysteresis, pulsed potentiation, and delayed depotentiation. Its parameters therefore derive from measured device data.

Each replicated composition cell is summarised by a \emph{parameter card}: the model-free half-enhancement time $t_{1/2,c}$, any identified stretch exponent $\beta_c$, the potentiation growth exponent $\alpha_c$, the measured peak ratio $R_{\mathrm{peak},c}$, and the pulse count $N_{\mathrm{peak},c}$ at that peak. The fading memory is the stretched-exponential (Kohlrausch) retention identified in \cref{ch:comparative},
\[
  \lambda_c(\Delta t) \;=\; \exp\!\big[-(\Delta t/\tau_c)^{\beta_c}\big],
\]
where $\tau_c=t_{1/2,c}/(\ln 2)^{1/\beta_c}$ is derived so that every card reproduces the primary, model-free cell-median half-time exactly. For cells without an identified stretch exponent, $\beta_c=1$ and therefore $\tau_c=t_{1/2,c}/\ln 2$. The write envelope is
\[
  \frac{G(N)}{G_0}=1+(R_{\mathrm{peak},c}-1)
  \left(\frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{peak},c}}\right)^{\alpha_c},
  \qquad 0\leq N\leq N_{\mathrm{peak},c}.
\]
It uses the measured baseline, exponent, peak, and pulse count rather than an unbounded power law. Where a measured train turns over after its peak, the node is capped at the peak: the post-peak recovery dynamics were not measured and are not extrapolated.

Driven by a rate-coded input $u_n \in [0,1]$ sampled at interval $\Delta t$, a device node accumulates pulse progress $p$ and relaxes along its measured kernel:
\[
  p^{(i)}_n=\operatorname{clip}\!\left[\sum_{k\geq 0} g^{(i)}_k\,
    \frac{\operatorname{clip}(w_i u_{n-k},0,1)}{N_{\mathrm{peak},i}},\,0,\,1\right],
  \qquad
  g^{(i)}_k=\lambda_i(k\,\Delta t)^{1/\alpha_i},
\]
\[
  x^{(i)}_n=(p^{(i)}_n)^{\alpha_i},\qquad
  \frac{G^{(i)}_n}{G^{(i)}_0}=1+(R_{\mathrm{peak},i}-1)\,x^{(i)}_n .
\]
Here $x_i\in[0,1]$ is the normalised enhancement, $w_i$ is a non-negative input mask, and one full-scale sample advances the pulse-progress coordinate by at most one effective pulse. That sample-to-pulse mapping is a modelling convention, not a measured circuit timing relation. The kernel $g=\lambda^{1/\alpha}$ makes the enhancement left by a single write decay exactly as the measured $\lambda_i$, and with no relaxation and unit input the node follows the measured envelope to its peak and cannot exceed it. Because the sum runs over the whole measured relaxation, a stretched decay is reproduced at any sampling interval. Applying the one-step factor $\lambda_i(\Delta t)$ repeatedly would instead compound into a plain exponential and, for $\beta<1$, shorten the simulated memory (for the lead cell at $\Delta t=\SI{1}{\second}$, to a half-life of \SI{11.5}{\second} instead of the measured \SI{19.2}{\second}). A node that reaches its peak is held there, with its stored history rescaled (\siTempRef{app:ch5_settings}). The internal state is assumed to be read sub-threshold without disturbance, as in the proof-of-concept device of \cref{ch:poc}. Every read-out is a single \emph{linear} ridge regression on the device states.

Write nonlinearity and fading memory were measured at different write amplitudes (\SI{3}{\volt} for potentiation and \SI{4}{\volt} for depotentiation; \cref{tab:ch4_protocols}). Combining them in one node assumes that the two regimes are compatible and probably underestimates the coupling between drive and retention. Both measurements also used a single, effectively common inter-pulse cadence, so the model does not resolve input timing below that cadence. The gold/\TMPE{} negative-polarity corpus of \cref{ch:comparative} measures integration and relaxation within single runs and finds both interval-dependent potentiation and written-depth-dependent relaxation. Because it uses another host, electrode, polarity, and read protocol, it does not provide parameters for the banks used here. The experiment needed for the lead composition is specified in \cref{sec:ch5_limitations}. Per-cell parameter cards and all simulation and read-out settings are tabulated in \siTempRef{app:ch5_settings}.

\paragraph{Component cards.} Read as circuit elements, the cards form the component table of \cref{tab:ch5_components}. Each cell is a low-pass element whose time constant is set by composition, whose corner frequency follows from its measured kernel, and whose tolerance is the within-cell scatter. For a single-exponential cell the corner is $f_c=1/(2\pi\tau_{RC})$. For a stretched cell it lies lower, because the long tail of the relaxation retains low frequencies, so the half-life alone does not fix the filter.

\section{Designed Filters from the Component Cards}\label{sec:ch5_filters}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Before any reservoir is assembled, each composition cell can be used on its own. Its relaxation kernel defines a causal low-pass filter whose corner frequency is fixed when the film is formulated (\cref{tab:ch5_components}). Two elementary circuits follow from it. A device subtracted from its own input forms a high-pass baseline remover, \(y=x-\mathrm{LP}_c(x)\). Two compositions subtracted from each other form a band-pass, \(y=\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{fast}}(x)-\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{slow}}(x)\), which gives no output for a constant input. Both are routine preprocessing steps for slow sensor signals.

Electrodermal activity (EDA) is the test case. Its phasic part, the skin-conductance responses (SCRs) that follow sympathetic arousal, rides on a tonic level that drifts over minutes. Conventional pipelines remove the tonic level with a high-pass filter near \SI{0.05}{\hertz} and then detect SCRs or measure power in the \SIrange{0.045}{0.25}{\hertz} band associated with sympathetic tone~\cite{PosadaQuintero2016EDASymp}. Three references frame the comparison: an $RC$ element with the same half-life as the device, which is the conventional component for the same job; a causal digital Butterworth phasic filter (\SIrange{0.05}{1}{\hertz}); and the unfiltered signal.

Each device is represented by its measured kernel \(\exp[-(t/\tau_c)^{\beta_c}]\), applied as a causal convolution at \SI{4}{\hertz} on the assumption that the conductance enhancement superposes. Every simulated device draws its own time constant from the measured within-cell scatter, \(\sigma(\ln t_{1/2})=\num{0.264}\); the $RC$ references are ideal. The composed nonlinear node of \cref{sec:ch5_model} gives the same ranking once its read-out gain is set for small signals (\siTempRef{app:ch5_filters}).

\paragraph{Design space.} All eight high-pass corners lie between \num{0.003} and \SI{0.041}{\hertz}, below the lower edge of the sympathetic band (\cref{fig:ch5_filterbank}a). The two fastest cells (\(t_{1/2}\approx\SI{2.7}{\second}\)) place the corner close to the conventional \SI{0.05}{\hertz} tonic cut-off. No cell is fast enough to set the upper edge of the band. A two-composition band-pass therefore passes \SIrange{0.002}{0.047}{\hertz}, which is the tonic range. For stretched cells, the low-pass corner also sits well below that of the $RC$ element with the same half-life (\PEO{}~0.6/0.09: \num{0.009} against \SI{0.032}{\hertz}), because the long tail of a stretched relaxation retains low frequencies. The half-life alone therefore does not specify a filter; its corner has to be read from the measured kernel, as in \cref{tab:ch5_components}.

\paragraph{Synthetic signals with known responses.} Synthetic sessions supply ground truth. They contain Bateman-shaped SCRs~\cite{BenedekKaernbach2010} at about two and seven per minute in alternating five-minute calm and aroused blocks, a tonic rise in the aroused blocks, minutes-scale drift, and either low noise, broadband noise, or sharp motion-like artefacts. Detection thresholds and lags are calibrated on 12 sessions and scored on 30 others (\cref{fig:ch5_filterbank}b,c). The fastest single-device high-pass detects SCRs with F1 \num{0.713}, \num{0.680}, and \num{0.676} in the three conditions, against \num{0.701}, \num{0.669}, and \num{0.647} for the digital filter; under artefacts it is higher in 29 of 30 sessions. The $RC$ element with the same half-life scores \num{0.712}, \num{0.677}, and \num{0.675}. The best two-composition band-pass reaches only \numrange{0.50}{0.54} and passes about twice as much tonic as phasic power. The unfiltered signal scores \num{0.690} on clean data, because peak prominence already discounts a slow baseline; filtering helps mainly against noise and artefacts.

\paragraph{WESAD recordings.} On WESAD, per-minute phasic power is compared between the stress and baseline segments of each subject (\cref{fig:ch5_filterbank}d). The single-device high-pass gives a within-subject AUC of \num{0.952} on the chest and \num{0.921} on the wrist, against \num{0.951} and \num{0.927} for the digital filter and \num{0.947} and \num{0.922} for a filter restricted to the sympathetic band. The per-minute features of the device and digital filters are closely correlated (Spearman \(\rho=\num{0.98}\) and \num{0.99}). The tonic level alone gives \num{0.915} and \num{0.885}. The device-minus-digital difference is \(+\num{0.001}\) on the chest (\(p=\num{0.94}\)) and \(-\num{0.006}\) on the wrist (2/15 subjects higher, \(p=\num{0.038}\)). The two-composition band-pass is lower at both sites (\num{0.903} and \num{0.880}).

\paragraph{Device against $RC$ element.} Across all eight cells, the measured kernel never improves on the $RC$ element with the same half-life (\siTempRef{tab:ch5_device_vs_rc}). The fastest cell relaxes as a simple exponential (\(\beta=\num{1.00}\)) and is therefore the same filter as its $RC$ counterpart. The two most stretched cells (\(\beta=\num{0.61}\)) are slightly worse on the chest (\(-\num{0.009}\) AUC; \(p=\num{0.013}\) and \num{0.038}). Used as a filter, a composition-selected device is an $RC$ element whose time constant is set at fabrication. Its case rests on how that time constant is obtained. A \SI{0.0825}{\square\centi\metre} two-terminal film reaches seconds with no separate capacitor, and impedance spectroscopy places the storage in the ionic response of the film (\cref{sec:ch4_eis}). In analogue CMOS, time constants of hundreds of milliseconds already need large capacitors or very small currents~\cite{IndiveriSandamirskaya2019}, and printed circuits have used printed low-pass filters for temporal processing, including printed EDA stress detection~\cite{Zhao2022PrintedEDA,Zhao2023PrintedFilters}. Footprint, voltage, stability, and tolerance were not compared against a printed $RC$ element realising the same time constant; that comparison would decide the practical value of the device.

\section{Reservoir Computing and the Common Metric Language}\label{sec:ch5_framework}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{The reservoir principle.} The remaining sections test the second use of the cards, as nodes of a reservoir. Reservoir computing uses a fixed dynamical system whose state is connected to a trained linear read-out \cite{Jaeger2001ESN,Maass2002LSM,LukoseviciusJaeger2009}. A physical reservoir requires fading memory, so its state retains recent input history, and a nonlinear response that maps inputs into features accessible to the read-out. \Cref{ch:comparative} measures both properties in these devices: stretched-exponential depotentiation supplies fading memory, and compressive potentiation supplies nonlinearity. Memristive and organic implementations have previously used the same combination \cite{Tanaka2019,Du2017,Zhong2021,Cucchi2021}. The behavioural models test whether the measured polymer-electrolyte response supplies that combination.

\paragraph{Architecture.} The reservoir is a spatial bank of $N$ \emph{independent} device nodes, each represented by the behavioural model of \cref{sec:ch5_model} and driven by a randomly masked copy of the input. With no inter-node recurrence, differences between banks arise from their intrinsic timescales, device-to-device scatter, and input masks rather than engineered connectivity. Each reservoir result is paired with a linear control: exponential moving averages (EMAs) of the input channels at the device half-lives, $\tau=t_{1/2}/\ln 2$, read out in the same way. The EMA bank has the time constants of the device bank but none of its nonlinearity or random masking. For Demonstration~A, the same single-composition node could instead be deployed as a time-multiplexed delay-feedback reservoir with virtual nodes along a delay line \cite{Appeltant2011}; the spatial bank is used throughout for consistency.

\paragraph{Common metrics.} Linear memory capacity $\mathrm{MC}_k$ and its total \cite{Jaeger2002STM} quantify the past input recoverable at each lag. \NARMA{} \cite{AtiyaParlos2000} provides a nonlinear system-identification benchmark, and the information-processing-capacity framework \cite{Dambre2012} separates linear and nonlinear contributions (\cref{fig:ch5_ipc}). Affective tasks are scored by class-balanced macro-F1. Robustness is tested by injecting the measured within-cell timescale spread into the bank (\cref{fig:ch5_robustness}); no common scatter estimate is assumed for $\alpha$ or $\beta$.

\section{Benchmark Tier: Capacity and System Identification}\label{sec:ch5_benchmarks}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Reservoir suitability is first evaluated without an application-specific task. Linear memory capacity measures fading memory, while information-processing capacity separates its linear and nonlinear components. The \NARMA{} system-identification task is used only to rank compositions, not to claim absolute performance. All three benchmarks use a random rate-coded input, $N$ device nodes, and a linear ridge read-out.

\paragraph{Statistical units.} Random input and bank seeds sample the algorithm's sensitivity to stochastic initialisation; they are not independent devices, experiments, or subjects. Seed-only benchmark comparisons therefore report means, standard deviations, and sign consistency without inferential $p$-values. Inferential paired tests are reserved for independent held-out subjects in the physiological-corpus analyses, or for independently fabricated devices in the experimental chapters. The complete distinction is recorded in \siTempRef{tab:ch5_settings}.

\paragraph{Memory capacity.} The linear memory capacity of Jaeger measures how much of the past input a reservoir can reconstruct: $\mathrm{MC}_k$ is the squared correlation between a ridge reconstruction of the input $k$ steps ago and its true value, and the total is the sum over all lags, bounded above by the number of nodes $N$ \cite{Jaeger2002STM}. \Cref{fig:ch5_mc_curve} compares two banks of equal size ($N=24$): a \emph{homogeneous} bank of jittered copies of the lead cell (\PEO{}~0.3/0.09) and a \emph{heterogeneous} bank spanning the replicated composition grid ($\tau\approx 4$--$30$\,s). Total capacity rises from $3.22\pm0.32$ to $4.44\pm0.45$ (means $\pm$ SD over ten random input/bank seeds), a ratio of $1.38\pm0.14$ and a paired gain of $1.22\pm0.41$ that is positive in all ten seed pairs. Most of the gain sits at the shortest lags: single-step recall rises from $\mathrm{MC}_1=0.41$ to $0.98$, whereas recall at lag~15 is similar ($0.08$ and $0.07$). Copies of one slow cell carry strongly correlated states, and the faster cells of the heterogeneous bank supply the short-lag information they lack.

\paragraph{Composition sweep.} The same benchmarks, run on single-composition banks, rank the compositions against one another (\cref{fig:ch5_composition_sweep}). The strongly stretched \PEO{}~0.6/0.09 cell ($\beta=0.61$) attains the highest single-composition memory capacity ($3.35 \pm 0.36$, mean $\pm$ SD over ten seeds) and the lowest error on the \NARMA{} system-identification benchmark \cite{AtiyaParlos2000} (NRMSE $0.614 \pm 0.018$); the lead cell \PEO{}~0.3/0.09 gives $2.96 \pm 0.26$ and $0.692 \pm 0.033$. A stretched relaxation is a distribution of time constants, so one stretched device already spreads its memory over several lags. The differences between neighbouring cells are algorithmic rankings, not experimental evidence of a unique optimum. The \NARMA{} numbers carry only \emph{relative} weight: the absolute error is high ($\approx 0.6$--$0.9$) because a bank of independent, non-recurrent nodes is a weak \NARMA{} reservoir.

\paragraph{Comparison with randomly distributed timescales.} A generic echo-state bank of the same size tests whether the measured timescales are individually important or whether their range is sufficient. A random bank spanning approximately the same range ($\tau=3$--$30$\,s) has a memory capacity of $4.97\pm0.78$, compared with $4.44\pm0.45$ for the device bank (ten matched seeds; the device is higher in four). Expanding the random range to $0.5$--$60$\,s raises the capacity to $5.56\pm0.74$ (the device is higher in one). The measured values are therefore not uniquely optimal; this control attributes the available linear memory to the range of fading times rather than to their exact distribution. The broader-range result is only a modelling prompt: the corresponding slow devices have not been measured.

\paragraph{Linear and nonlinear capacity.} Memory capacity counts only linearly recoverable information. The information-processing-capacity framework of Dambre et al.\ decomposes total capacity into orthogonal polynomial degrees: degree-one terms give linear capacity, while degree-two terms measure nonlinear self and cross-lag transformations; the total is bounded by the node count $N$ \cite{Dambre2012}. \Cref{fig:ch5_ipc} reports this decomposition. The homogeneous bank has total capacity $5.18\pm0.34$, comprising $3.61$ linear and $1.57$ nonlinear capacity. The heterogeneous bank reaches $9.77\pm0.73$, comprising $5.06$ linear and $4.71$ nonlinear capacity; its nonlinear increment of $3.13$ is positive in all ten seed pairs. Of the heterogeneous nonlinear capacity, $2.55$ is from self terms and $2.16$ from cross-lag products. Like the linear comparison, this is a sensitivity result for the composed behavioural model rather than an experimental population estimate.

\section{Physiological Signals as a Test Bed}\label{sec:ch5_affect}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Affective computing uses physiological signals to infer emotional and physiological state~\cite{Picard1997}; here its signals and labels serve as a test bed for the device models. The device timescales, from a few seconds to tens of seconds, overlap the phasic electrodermal, respiration, and heart-rate-variability bands without time rescaling (\cref{tab:ch5_bands}). These signals also contain several timescales at once: phasic electrodermal responses, respiration and heart-rate-variability rhythms over seconds, and tonic levels drifting over minutes~\cite{Boucsein2012EDA,TaskForce1996HRV,ChourpiliadisBhardwaj2022}. \Cref{fig:ch5_tau_coverage} places the measured relaxation time of each composition on the same axis as these physiological bands and shows the minutes-scale range not reached by composition alone.

\paragraph{Extending the model to tonic timescales.} Raising the write amplitude lengthens fading memory in the measurements of \cref{ch:comparative}. Adding modelled nodes at $\tau=120$ and \SI{300}{\second} changes little: random-input capacity beyond \SI{75}{\second} rises from $0.82$ to $0.97$ ($9/10$ seed pairs), total capacity is unchanged ($4.67$ and $4.64$), and slow-band physiological reconstruction at delays up to \SI{120}{\second} rises by only $+0.003\pm0.022$ $R^2$ ($5/10$ draws; \siTempRef{app:ch5_controls}). The stretched tails of the measured cells already carry much of this long-lag memory. The extension is hypothetical in any case, because no minutes-scale cell was measured and the drive-amplitude data do not identify these two time constants.

The experiments that follow use the Wearable Stress and Affect Detection corpus (\WESAD{}) of Schmidt et al.\ \cite{Schmidt2018WESAD}: fifteen subjects wearing a chest sensor that records electrodermal activity, respiration, temperature, and an electrocardiogram, with periods labelled baseline, stress, or amusement. \WESAD{} is used in two complementary ways: as a source of real physiological streams whose multi-lag temporal context the reservoir must encode (\cref{sec:ch5_physio}), and as a downstream affective-classification task (\cref{sec:ch5_wesad}).

\section{Physiological Temporal-Context Reconstruction}\label{sec:ch5_physio}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The benchmarks of \cref{sec:ch5_benchmarks} show that a spread of device timescales broadens memory on a random input. This section tests the same property on the multi-timescale physiological signals introduced in \cref{sec:ch5_affect}, isolating temporal-context encoding before turning to affect labels (\cref{sec:ch5_wesad}).

The reservoir is run continuously over each \WESAD{} session at $\Delta t = \SI{1}{\second}$ on four chest channels (electrodermal activity, respiration, temperature, and an electrocardiogram-derived heart rate), and a linear read-out reconstructs each channel at delays of $1$, $3$, $8$, $20$ and \SI{45}{\second}. These delays span fast phasic and beat-to-beat context, respiration- and HF-HRV-scale context, and tonic, LF-HRV-scale context~\cite{Boucsein2012EDA,TaskForce1996HRV}. Evaluation is leave-one-subject-out over all fifteen subjects, with $N=48$ nodes per bank.

Preprocessing is causal. ECG filtering and RR-interval assignment use only completed beats; resampling uses the nearest past label. Each channel is mapped to $(0,1)$ by a monotone transform fitted to the first \SI{60}{\second} only: the calibration-prefix 5th and 95th percentiles map to 0.25 and 0.75, without hard clipping later values. Thus neither later held-out values nor affect labels set the scale. The reported out-of-sample $R^2$ uses the training-subject mean as its deployable null predictor, clips reconstructed normalised physiology to $[0,1]$, and weights each held-out subject equally.

\Cref{tab:ch5_physio} reports the held-out reconstruction quality. A memoryless bank gives $R^2 = 0.636$, compared with $0.646$ for instantaneous input. A single-timescale device bank raises $R^2$ to $0.679$--$0.684$, and the heterogeneous composition bank reaches $0.707$: $+0.061$ over instantaneous input and $+0.023$ over the stronger same-size homogeneous control. The heterogeneous-minus-fast and heterogeneous-minus-slow differences are each positive in $14/15$ subjects (Holm-adjusted $p=0.0067$). The fast homogeneous bank is strongest at the shortest delays and the slow homogeneous bank at the longest (\cref{fig:ch5_physio}). A linear EMA bank at the eight cell half-lives, with no device nonlinearity or random mask, reaches $0.742$ and exceeds the heterogeneous device bank in all fifteen subjects ($-0.035$; Holm-adjusted $p=1.8\times10^{-4}$); a single EMA at the lead half-life gives $0.688$. The useful content of the bank is therefore its set of time constants, and the nonlinear write and random masks lower the reconstruction.

\section{WESAD Affective Classification}\label{sec:ch5_wesad}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The downstream task is affective-state classification from the \WESAD{} physiological streams with a leave-one-subject-out linear read-out. The reservoir and the \SI{15}{\second} causal prediction smoother run on the complete physical timeline; samples with undefined affect labels are excluded only from fitting and scoring. This prevents the smoother from joining labelled blocks separated by long protocol gaps. The study that introduced \WESAD{} reports, with engineered window features and conventional classifiers under leave-one-subject-out validation, chest macro-F1 of \numrange{0.725}{0.744} for the three classes and \numrange{0.904}{0.915} for stress versus non-stress, and \num{0.861} for stress versus non-stress from the wrist~\cite{Schmidt2018WESAD}. The streaming linear read-out of this chapter reaches \num{0.562}, \num{0.799}, and \num{0.756} on the same three tasks (\cref{tab:ch5_sota}). The formulations differ (per-step streaming against windowed features), but a gap of \numrange{0.1}{0.2} is too large to attribute to formulation alone: the device models do not approach an engineered-feature pipeline on these labels.

\section{Continuous Monitoring Under Deployment Conditions}\label{sec:ch5_robust}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The sustained \WESAD{} states can often be classified from the instantaneous signal level, as shown by the static-feature baseline. Wearable measurements also contain sensor noise and motion artefacts, particularly in wrist-worn electrodermal and photoplethysmographic signals. Fading memory implements temporal low-pass filtering and may therefore reduce sensitivity to transient artefacts. The tests below add synthetic noise, measured-motion gating, and an independent sensor corpus.

\Cref{fig:ch5_robust_monitoring} tests a continuous binary stress detector (stress versus baseline plus amusement, read out at every step, leave-one-subject-out, with the same linear read-out used throughout). On clean streams, macro-F1 ranges from $0.781$ to $0.807$. At $\sigma=0.4$, instantaneous and memoryless reads fall to $0.641$ and $0.649$, while the homogeneous and heterogeneous memory banks retain $0.818$ and $0.811$. The heterogeneous-minus-instantaneous subject difference is $+0.171$ ($12/15$ positive, paired Wilcoxon $p=0.015$). Clean false-alarm rates are similar (instantaneous $0.094$, heterogeneous $0.089$ seed-mean). Transition-window F1 changes from $0.832$ to $0.840$, but the subject difference is not resolved ($6/15$ positive, $p=0.58$); all banks have zero median onset latency under the five-second stable-call definition. Temporal smoothing is useful under corruption, not for earlier detection in this protocol.

\paragraph{Accelerometer-gated motion artefact.} Because the preceding corruption is synthetic, a second test gates its amplitude with the measured chest accelerometer. At the strongest gating, the instantaneous read falls to $0.702$, the heterogeneous bank retains $0.811$, and the EMA reaches $0.809$. Both gains over the instantaneous read are $+0.111$; the EMA gain is resolved ($p=0.035$) and the heterogeneous gain is not ($p=0.055$; \siTempRef{app:ch5_robust}). The data-driven control therefore supports temporal filtering under measured-motion corruption, but not a separate device-model advantage.

\paragraph{Classical-filter control.} A single exponential moving average at the lead half-life reaches macro-F1 $0.804$ at $\sigma=0.4$, compared with $0.641$ for instantaneous input; its paired subject gain is $+0.170$ ($p=0.022$). A linear EMA bank at the eight half-lives reaches $0.808$, with no resolved gain over the single EMA ($+0.003$, $p=0.35$). The heterogeneous device bank reaches $0.811$ and does not differ from the EMA bank ($-0.002$, $p=0.64$; \siTempRef{tab:ch5_filter}). Robustness is therefore attributable to temporal low-pass filtering at these time constants, which the device bank performs as well as the linear control and no better.

\section{Toward a Worn Device: Sensor Site and Operating Budget}\label{sec:ch5_deploy}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The robustness of the previous section is a laboratory statement until two further questions are addressed: whether the advantage survives a consumer-grade sensor, and what can be said about an operating budget. The first is tested with the \WESAD{} wrist recordings. For the second, only a reference calculation is possible because the simulated \PEO{} nodes were not measured as a wired bank.

\paragraph{Sensor site.} \WESAD{} records each session with both a chest strap and an Empatica E4 wrist band. The wrist band has fewer and noisier channels: skin conductance, skin temperature, and photoplethysmography-derived heart rate, without respiration. On clean wrist data (\cref{tab:ch5_wrist}), instantaneous and heterogeneous scores are $0.724$ and $0.756$. At $\sigma=0.4$ they are $0.615$ and $0.769$; the paired subject gain is $+0.117$ ($10/15$ positive, $p=0.035$). The corresponding chest scores are $0.641$ and $0.811$. The linear EMA bank gives $0.773$ clean and $0.755$ at $\sigma=0.4$ on the wrist, and the device bank does not differ from it ($-0.013$, $p=0.38$). Clean wrist false-alarm rates are not improved (instantaneous $0.112$, heterogeneous $0.106$), so the supported benefit is robustness under injected corruption rather than fewer clean-stream alarms.

\paragraph{Operating-budget reference.} The parameter count is exact for the simulated architecture: the device dynamics are fixed, and the fitted $(N{+}1)\times K$ read-out contains $75$ weights for $N=24$, $K=3$, or $147$ for $N=48$. The read-out also requires $NK$ multiply--accumulates per step. Energy is less certain. Recalculation of one proof-of-concept \SY{}/\Hybrane{}/\LiTf{} junction gives \SIrange{0.084}{1.55}{\micro\joule} across its positive write sequence (median \SI{1.08}{\micro\joule}). Multiplying that median by 24 nodes gives a reference core cost of \SI{25.9}{\micro\joule} per update, or \SI{25.9}{\micro\watt} at one update per second; the corresponding 48-node value is \SI{51.8}{\micro\watt}.

These products are not power predictions for the proposed reservoir. They transfer a \SI{1}{\volt} result from one large-area \Hybrane{} junction to \PEO{} models identified at \SIrange{3}{4}{\volt}, without measured bank wiring, read energy, or peripheral circuits. They exclude the sensor analogue front-end, analogue-to-digital conversion, addressing, and control, and are neither an upper nor a lower bound. The device response remains faster than the sub-hertz physiological signals considered here, and its relaxation would perform the temporal filtering during the write/read cycle. The assumptions are tabulated in \siTempRef{app:ch5_deploy}.

\section{Matching Device and Task Timescales}\label{sec:ch5_designrules}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Choosing a cell.} For a designed filter, the target corner frequency selects the composition (\cref{tab:ch5_components}). The two fastest cells, with corners near \SI{0.04}{\hertz}, are the baseline removers for electrodermal activity; slower cells smooth over tens of seconds and pass the tonic band. Because stretching lowers the corner, a stretched cell must be chosen by its corner frequency, not by its half-life. Corners above about \SI{0.04}{\hertz} lie outside the replicated grid; the faster host and anion variants of \cref{ch:comparative} (\TMPE{}, \(t_{1/2}\approx\SI{4}{\second}\); TFSI, \(\approx\SI{1}{\second}\)) indicate where faster elements might come from, at survey-level evidence.

\paragraph{Reservoir banks.} Performance depends on the overlap between device and task timescales (\cref{fig:ch5_tau_coverage}). In the single-composition sweep, the strongly stretched \PEO{}~0.6/0.09 cell gives the highest memory capacity for the seconds-scale benchmark (\cref{fig:ch5_composition_sweep}). For the multi-lag physiological task, no single cell covers the full delay range, and the heterogeneous bank gives the highest device-bank score, below the linear EMA bank at the same half-lives (\cref{tab:ch5_physio}).

\paragraph{Effect of timescale breadth.} \Cref{tab:ch5_summary} separates fading memory from timescale breadth and from the device nonlinearity. On random input, breadth raises total linear memory ($+1.22\pm0.41$, positive in $10/10$ seeds) and nonlinear information-processing capacity. On real physiology, heterogeneity gives a $+0.023$ context-reconstruction gain over the stronger homogeneous control, but a linear EMA bank at the same half-lives does better than either. On sustained three-class labels the two device banks tie, and under corruption the homogeneous bank is marginally stronger. The task, not diversity alone, determines the useful distribution.

When one slow timescale dominates, as in the sustained stress labels and the stress-monitoring task, the homogeneous and heterogeneous banks are within $0.008$ macro-F1 of each other at every site and noise level. The available variation comes from composition and device-to-device scatter; chemistry and drive amplitude provide additional, sample-limited axes in \cref{ch:comparative}.

\paragraph{Operating point and integration.} The models apply in the measured sub-kilohertz regime, which covers the physiological signals used here but not fast logic. The assumed sub-threshold, state-preserving read sets the read-voltage and read-disturb limits for a circuit implementation. \Cref{fig:ch5_robustness} varies timescale-only jitter. Its primary value, \(\sigma(\ln t_{1/2})\approx\num{0.264}\), is the median within-cell substrate-level spread on the screened replicated grid (\siTempRef{app:ch5_scatter}). The observed per-cell estimates span $0.089$--$1.018$, so the wider sweep is a sensitivity analysis rather than a fitted uncertainty distribution. The legacy single-exponential fit gives a larger \(\sigma(\ln\tau)\approx\num{0.85}\), retained only as an upper stress-test value. No measured common jitter is applied to $\alpha$ or $\beta$. At zero timescale scatter, copies of one card are more redundant; the heterogeneous bank still differs through composition-dependent card parameters.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Limitations and Scope Conditions}\label{sec:ch5_limitations}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{In silico.} The demonstrations are simulations. Each node is the measured $\phi \otimes \lambda \otimes f$ behavioural model of \cref{sec:ch5_model}, not a bench circuit. Every figure caption names the data behind it. Neither a wired filter nor a wired reservoir was built.

\paragraph{Designed filters.} The filter results assume that the conductance enhancement superposes and that the measured large-signal decay also governs small signals around a written state. The composed node predicts a faster small-signal kernel when $\alpha<1$ (\siTempRef{app:ch5_filters}); a small write superposed on a written state would decide between the two. The synthetic generator uses chosen parameter ranges rather than a fitted corpus, and the WESAD stress segment (a speaking task) may add movement-related phasic power; this affects all filters alike. The $RC$ references are ideal components, and footprint, energy, and tolerance were not compared against a fabricated $RC$ element.

\paragraph{The composition assumption.} As detailed in \cref{sec:ch5_model}, write nonlinearity and fading memory were measured separately, at different write amplitudes and one inter-pulse cadence. Combining them assumes that the two regimes are compatible; even the direction and magnitude of the omitted coupling are uncertain for the silver/\PEO{} system. In a sensitivity analysis, each node's retention was rescaled with its write exponent over $\kappa=-0.5$ to $+0.5$. The mean heterogeneous-to-homogeneous capacity ratio stays between $1.29$ and $1.42$, with the heterogeneous bank higher in every seed pair (\siTempRef{app:ch5_controls}). The gold/\TMPE{} negative-polarity measurements provide an external directional reference, $\rho=+0.46$ between write depth and decay half-time across $28$ single-run traces. The nominal cards already have a stronger across-card rank association ($\rho\approx+0.57$), so the central external estimate maps to $\kappa^{\star}=0$ and its upper confidence limit to about $+0.31$; the capacity ratio is $1.38$ and $1.33$ at these points. Because host, electrode, polarity, and protocol differ, this is a sensitivity anchor rather than validation of the composed silver model. A matched-amplitude experiment on the lead composition that varies pulse count and inter-pulse interval together is required.

\paragraph{Operating regime and read-out.} The model is identified only in the measured sub-kilohertz regime; fast temporal tasks are outside its scope. Every read-out is a single ridge regression because a nonlinear read-out could supply the task nonlinearity independently of the reservoir. The read is assumed to be sub-threshold and state-preserving.

\paragraph{Heterogeneity axes.} The quantitative comparisons span the replicated \PEO{}/\LiTf{} composition grid; the single recovered \PEO{}~0.15 substrate is excluded. Chemistry and drive amplitude enter only as secondary controls because their matched cells contain at most two substrates (\cref{ch:comparative}); Demonstration~B is a proof of principle for composition heterogeneity, not a powered comparison across chemistries. The cards index composition, but thickness and other fabrication variables are not fully separable from that design. The available covariate checks do not explain the composition association, yet they cannot prove composition is the sole cause (\cref{fig:ch4_thickness_control}, \cref{sec:ch4_materials}).

\paragraph{Corpora, protocol, and negative controls.} The affective results use \WESAD{} as the main corpus, and noise robustness is replicated on the PhysioNet Non-EEG dataset (\cref{sec:ch5_robust}). A broader multi-corpus study remains future work. In \WESAD{}, the experimental blocks follow a fixed protocol rather than a counterbalanced order. Affect label, elapsed time, and slow session drift are therefore partly entangled: causal prefix calibration prevents future-sample leakage, but it cannot establish that a classifier is specific to affect rather than protocol position. The sustained block labels also do not provide independent ground truth for physiological onset, so the transition and latency analyses quantify agreement with protocol boundaries, not the onset of an internal state.

The strongest robustness results use synthetic, channel-wise corruption and should be read as controlled stress tests. The measured-motion gate is a more realistic but weaker control and does not resolve a heterogeneous-over-instantaneous advantage. The linear controls bound the device-specific contribution. A linear EMA bank at the same half-lives exceeds the device bank on physiological reconstruction and matches it under synthetic corruption on the chest and wrist (\siTempRef{tab:ch5_filter}); the device banks exceed the linear controls only on the sustained three-class labels and, by $0.023$, under corruption on the Non-EEG corpus. Timescale heterogeneity does not improve the final-label score (\cref{sec:ch5_wesad}).

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Summary}\label{sec:ch5_conclusions}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Read as component cards, the replicated compositions supply time constants of about 4 to \SI{28}{\second} and low-pass corners of \SIrange{0.002}{0.04}{\hertz}. Used as a designed filter, the fastest cell removes the tonic level of electrodermal signals as well as a digital filter (synthetic SCR detection F1 \num{0.713} against \num{0.701}; WESAD chest stress-versus-baseline AUC \num{0.952} against \num{0.951}) and behaves as an $RC$ element with the same half-life. The measured stretch never improves on that element, and two-composition band-passes fall below the phasic band.

Behavioural models fitted to the measurements of \cref{ch:comparative} also support reservoir simulations with a linear read-out. Each node follows its measured relaxation kernel and is bounded by the measured potentiation peak and pulse count. Under this model, the heterogeneous bank raises total linear memory from $3.22$ to $4.44$ ($10/10$ seeds) and mean information-processing capacity from $5.18$ to $9.77$. On \WESAD{}, a lead-cell reservoir scores $0.671$ on windowed binary EDA, below a static control ($0.714$). Streaming three-class macro-F1 is $0.562$ for both heterogeneous and homogeneous banks, above linear EMA controls ($0.508$). Under synthetic corruption, the heterogeneous bank retains $0.811$ while instantaneous input falls to $0.641$, and a linear EMA bank at the same half-lives retains $0.808$.

The additional value of a timescale distribution depends on the task and metric. It is large for random-input memory and nonlinear capacity, modest but subject-resolved for multi-lag physiological reconstruction ($+0.023$ $R^2$ over the slow homogeneous control), and absent for sustained \WESAD{} labels. On reconstruction, a linear EMA bank at the same half-lives does better than any device bank ($0.742$ against $0.707$), so the time constants themselves carry most of the benefit. Noise robustness replicates on the independent Non-EEG corpus and represents temporal filtering rather than a device-specific accuracy advantage.

The chapter therefore finds a practical role for the composition-set time constant as a designed filter element, and little added value from random banks over linear filters at the same time constants. The next experimental steps follow from that role: replaying recorded electrodermal signals through a fabricated device, measuring the relaxation of a small write on a written state, and comparing a device with a printed $RC$ element of the same time constant. A matched-amplitude pulse protocol and a replicated chemistry study would then test the model assumptions and extend the range of available time constants.
