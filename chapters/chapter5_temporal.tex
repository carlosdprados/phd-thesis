\section{Introduction and Scope}\label{sec:ch5_intro}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

\Cref{ch:poc,ch:comparative} established and quantified the slow, volatile response of these polymer-electrolyte composites. Here, the measured response is represented by behavioural models and evaluated as a temporal-computing resource. A reservoir maps a time-varying input onto a high-dimensional dynamical state from which a linear read-out can recover temporal information.

The central comparison is between a bank drawn from one composition and a bank spanning several measured timescales. The latter should help when a target depends on several lags, but need not improve a task dominated by one timescale. Wearable \emph{affective computing} (the recognition of emotional and physiological state) provides a relevant test because its signals contain both fast and slow components. The evaluation proceeds from reservoir benchmarks to physiological streams, \WESAD{} classification, continuous valence/arousal tracking, and tests with sensor noise and wrist-band data.

All results in this chapter are \emph{in silico}; no new devices were fabricated. Each node is a compact behavioural model parameterised by the fits in \cref{ch:comparative} (\cref{sec:ch5_model}), and every read-out is a single linear ridge regression. Demonstration~A tests whether one composition suffices for a single-timescale feature. Demonstration~B tests whether a composition-diverse bank improves multi-timescale tasks.

\section{Measured Behavioural Model}\label{sec:ch5_model}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Each composition cell is reduced to a compact behavioural model and used as one reservoir node. The model is fitted only to the three common measurements of \cref{ch:comparative}: hysteresis, pulsed potentiation, and delayed depotentiation. Its parameters therefore derive from measured device data.

Each composition cell is summarised by a \emph{parameter card}: the write nonlinearity $\phi_c$ from the potentiation curves, the fading-memory law $\lambda_c$ from the depotentiation curves, and an associated device-to-device spread. The fading memory is the stretched-exponential (Kohlrausch) retention identified in \cref{ch:comparative},
\[
  \lambda_c(\Delta t) \;=\; \exp\!\big[-(\Delta t/\tau_c)^{\beta_c}\big],
\]
where $\tau_c$ and $\beta_c$ are the per-cell relaxation time and stretch exponent; for the few cells without an identified stretched fit, a single exponential reproducing the model-free half-life is used instead ($\tau_c = t_{1/2}/\ln 2$, $\beta_c = 1$). The write nonlinearity is the compressive, saturating build-up of conductance with pulse number measured in \cref{ch:comparative}, parametrised by its log--log growth exponent $\alpha_c$ and its peak enhancement.

Driven by a rate-coded input $u_n \in [0,1]$ sampled at interval $\Delta t$, a single device node updates as
\[
  x^{(i)}_n \;=\; \lambda_i\, x^{(i)}_{n-1} \;+\; \big[\max(w_i\, u_n,\,0)\big]^{\alpha_i},
  \qquad \lambda_i = \exp\!\big[-(\Delta t/\tau_i)^{\beta_i}\big],
\]
so that the node integrates a compressive write of its masked input and then leaks according to the measured retention $\lambda_i$ over the sample interval; $w_i$ is the reservoir input mask. The internal state is read sub-threshold and is assumed to be state-preserving, as in the proof-of-concept device of \cref{ch:poc}. Every read-out is a single \emph{linear} ridge regression on the device states.

The model has two measurement-related constraints. First, write nonlinearity and fading memory were measured at different write amplitudes (\SI{3}{\volt} for potentiation and \SI{4}{\volt} for depotentiation; \cref{tab:ch4_protocols}). Combining them in one node assumes that the two regimes are compatible and probably underestimates the coupling between drive and retention. Second, both measurements used a single, effectively common inter-pulse cadence, so the model does not resolve input timing below that cadence. The gold/\TMPE{} negative-polarity corpus of \cref{ch:comparative} measures integration and forgetting within single runs and finds both interval-dependent potentiation and written-depth-dependent relaxation. Because it uses another host, electrode, polarity, and read protocol, it does not provide parameters for the banks used here. The experiment needed for the lead composition is specified in \cref{sec:ch5_limitations}. Per-cell parameter cards and all simulation and read-out settings are tabulated in \siTempRef{app:ch5_settings}.

\section{Reservoir Computing and the Common Metric Language}\label{sec:ch5_framework}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{The reservoir principle.} Reservoir computing emerged from the observation that a fixed, randomly-parametrised dynamical system (the \emph{reservoir}) can perform useful temporal computation provided only that a \emph{linear} read-out is trained on its state, leaving the dynamics themselves untrained \cite{Jaeger2001ESN,Maass2002LSM,LukoseviciusJaeger2009}. Two properties make a physical system a useful reservoir candidate: a \emph{fading memory} of past inputs, so that the state reflects recent history but eventually forgets, and a \emph{nonlinear} response, so that the state mixes inputs in ways a linear read-out can exploit. These are precisely the two properties characterised in \cref{ch:comparative} (the stretched-exponential depotentiation supplies the fading memory, the compressive potentiation supplies the nonlinearity), which is what makes a bank of these devices a natural candidate substrate for reservoir computing. The same recognition has driven a wave of memristive and organic reservoir demonstrations \cite{Tanaka2019,Du2017,Zhong2021,Cucchi2021}, of which the polymer-electrolyte devices studied here are a low-power, biocompatible instance.

\paragraph{Architecture.} The reservoir is a spatial bank of $N$ \emph{independent} device nodes, each represented by the behavioural model of \cref{sec:ch5_model} and driven by a randomly masked copy of the input. With no inter-node recurrence, differences between banks arise from their intrinsic timescales, device-to-device scatter, and input masks rather than engineered connectivity. For Demonstration~A, the same single-composition node could instead be deployed as a time-multiplexed delay-feedback reservoir with virtual nodes along a delay line \cite{Appeltant2011}; the spatial bank is used throughout for consistency.

\paragraph{Common metrics.} Linear memory capacity $\mathrm{MC}_k$ and its total \cite{Jaeger2002STM} quantify the past input recoverable at each lag. \NARMA{} \cite{AtiyaParlos2000} provides a nonlinear system-identification benchmark, and the information-processing-capacity framework \cite{Dambre2012} separates linear and nonlinear contributions (\cref{fig:ch5_ipc}). Affective tasks are scored by class-balanced macro-F1. Robustness is tested by injecting the measured device-to-device and cycle-to-cycle spread into the bank (\cref{fig:ch5_robustness}).

\section{Benchmark Tier: Capacity and System Identification}\label{sec:ch5_benchmarks}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Reservoir suitability is first evaluated without an application-specific task. Linear memory capacity measures fading memory, while information-processing capacity separates its linear and nonlinear components. The \NARMA{} system-identification task is used only to rank compositions, not to claim absolute performance. All three benchmarks use a random rate-coded input, $N$ device nodes, and a linear ridge read-out.

\paragraph{Memory capacity.} The linear memory capacity of Jaeger measures how much of the past input a reservoir can reconstruct: $\mathrm{MC}_k$ is the squared correlation between a ridge reconstruction of the input $k$ steps ago and its true value, and the total is the sum over all lags, bounded above by the number of nodes $N$ \cite{Jaeger2002STM}. \Cref{fig:ch5_mc_curve} compares two banks of equal size ($N=24$): a \emph{homogeneous} bank of jittered copies of the lead cell (\PEO{}~0.3/0.09, differing only by device-to-device scatter) and a \emph{heterogeneous} bank spanning the composition grid ($\tau$ from $\approx 3$ to $\approx 26$\,s). The heterogeneous bank broadens the memory profile and raises total capacity from $4.2$ to $6.4$ (means over ten random input/bank seeds), a factor of $1.54 \pm 0.10$. Single-step recall rises from $\mathrm{MC}_1=0.51$ to $1.00$, with most of the gain at short-to-intermediate lags. The increase occurs in all ten seeds (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank $p=0.002$, the minimum possible at this sample size). Because the nodes are independent, the increase comes from their timescale distribution, device scatter, and input masks.

\paragraph{Composition sweep and the single-node optimum.} The same benchmarks, run on single-composition banks, rank the compositions against one another and so test the Demonstration-A claim that one cell suffices for a single-timescale task. \Cref{fig:ch5_composition_sweep} shows that the lead cell \PEO{}~0.3/0.09 attains the highest single-composition memory capacity ($3.98 \pm 0.19$, mean $\pm$ SD over ten seeds), while the neighbouring \PEO{}~0.3/0.045 cell is marginally better on the \NARMA{} system-identification benchmark \cite{AtiyaParlos2000} (NRMSE $0.587 \pm 0.019$ against the lead's $0.616 \pm 0.031$, a close second). Both optima fall in the low-\PEO{} row that \cref{ch:comparative} identifies as carrying the longer fading-memory times, so the benchmark ranking is consistent with the composition result, not an artefact of the simulation. This supports the Demonstration-A statement: for a single-timescale feature, the lead composition is the memory-capacity optimum and a heterogeneous bank is unnecessary. The \NARMA{} numbers carry only \emph{relative} weight: the absolute error is high ($\approx 0.6$--$0.8$) because a bank of independent, non-recurrent nodes is a weak \NARMA{} reservoir, so the stronger evidence here comes from the capacity measures above (\cref{fig:ch5_mc_curve,fig:ch5_ipc}) and \NARMA{} serves only to corroborate the composition ranking, a limitation a recurrent or delay-feedback architecture would relax.

\paragraph{Comparison with randomly distributed timescales.} A generic echo-state bank of the same size tests whether the measured timescales are individually important or whether their range is sufficient. A random bank spanning the same range as the compositions ($\tau\approx 3$--$26$\,s) has a memory capacity of $6.7$, compared with $6.4$ for the device bank ($p=0.05$). Expanding the random range to $0.5$--$60$\,s raises the capacity to $7.6$ ($p=0.002$). The measured $\tau$ values are therefore not uniquely optimal; the relevant property is the range of timescales. The broader-range control also indicates that extending this range, for example through the drive-amplitude dependence measured in \cref{ch:comparative}, could increase capacity further.

\paragraph{Linear and nonlinear capacity.} Memory capacity counts only linearly recoverable information. The information-processing-capacity framework of Dambre et al.\ decomposes total capacity into orthogonal polynomial degrees: degree-one terms give linear capacity, while degree-two terms measure nonlinear self and cross-lag transformations; the total is bounded by the node count $N$ \cite{Dambre2012}. \Cref{fig:ch5_ipc} reports this decomposition. The homogeneous bank has a nonlinear capacity of $2.7$ within a total of $7.1$, showing that compressive potentiation contributes a nonlinear response in addition to fading memory. The heterogeneous bank raises the total capacity to $11.6$ (paired Wilcoxon $p=0.002$), with increases in both components. Almost all nonlinear capacity comes from degree-two self terms; cross-lag products are negligible, as expected for an independent, non-recurrent bank. The total remains below the $N$ bound for both banks.

\section{Affective Computing}\label{sec:ch5_affect}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Affective computing uses physiological signals to infer emotional and physiological state~\cite{Picard1997}. The device timescales, from a few seconds to tens of seconds, overlap the phasic electrodermal, respiration, and heart-rate-variability bands without time rescaling (\cref{tab:ch5_bands}). These signals also contain several timescales at once: phasic electrodermal responses, respiration and heart-rate-variability rhythms over seconds, and tonic levels drifting over minutes~\cite{Boucsein2012EDA,TaskForce1996HRV,ChourpiliadisBhardwaj2022}. \Cref{fig:ch5_tau_coverage} places the measured relaxation time of each composition on the same axis as these physiological bands and shows the minutes-scale range not reached by composition alone.

\paragraph{Extending the model to tonic timescales.} Raising the write amplitude lengthens fading memory in the measurements of \cref{ch:comparative}. Adding modelled, drive-boosted minutes-scale nodes nearly doubles recall beyond \SI{75}{\second} and raises total memory capacity from $6.4$ to $7.0$. On the physiological streams, reconstruction at delays up to \SI{120}{\second} improves by $+0.019$ in held-out $R^2$ (paired $p=0.002$, all ten seeds; \siTempRef{app:ch5_controls}). This is a modelled extrapolation because no minutes-scale cell was measured. A drive-amplitude experiment at these timescales is required to test it.

The experiments that follow use the Wearable Stress and Affect Detection corpus (\WESAD{}) of Schmidt et al.\ \cite{Schmidt2018WESAD}: fifteen subjects wearing a chest sensor that records electrodermal activity, respiration, temperature, and an electrocardiogram, with periods labelled baseline, stress, or amusement. \WESAD{} is used in two complementary ways: as a source of real physiological streams whose multi-lag temporal context the reservoir must encode (\cref{sec:ch5_physio}), and as a downstream affective-classification task (\cref{sec:ch5_wesad}).

\section{Physiological Temporal-Context Reconstruction}\label{sec:ch5_physio}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The benchmarks of \cref{sec:ch5_benchmarks} prove that a spread of device timescales broadens memory on a \emph{random} input. This section puts that property to a more demanding test on the real, intrinsically multi-timescale affective signals introduced in \cref{sec:ch5_affect}, isolating the multi-timescale \emph{encoding} question before turning to final labels (\cref{sec:ch5_wesad}).

The reservoir is run continuously over each \WESAD{} session at $\Delta t = \SI{1}{\second}$ on four chest channels (electrodermal activity, respiration, temperature, and an electrocardiogram-derived heart rate), and a linear read-out reconstructs each channel at delays of $1$, $3$, $8$, $20$ and \SI{45}{\second}. These delays span fast phasic and beat-to-beat context, respiration- and HF-HRV-scale context, and tonic, LF-HRV-scale context~\cite{Boucsein2012EDA,TaskForce1996HRV}. Evaluation is leave-one-subject-out over all fifteen subjects, with $N=48$ nodes per bank.

\Cref{tab:ch5_physio} reports the held-out reconstruction quality. A memoryless bank barely improves on instantaneous input ($R^2 = 0.674$ against $0.673$). A single-timescale memory raises $R^2$ to about $0.74$, and the heterogeneous composition bank reaches $0.756$: $+0.084$ over instantaneous input and $+0.012$ over the best same-size homogeneous control. The heterogeneous bank wins for all fifteen subjects (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank $p<10^{-4}$). In the band-resolved results, the fast homogeneous bank performs best at the shortest delays and the slow homogeneous bank at the longest. The heterogeneous bank gives the highest single-bank score across the full delay range (\cref{fig:ch5_physio}).

\section{WESAD Affective Classification}\label{sec:ch5_wesad}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The downstream task is affective-state classification from the \WESAD{} physiological streams with a leave-one-subject-out linear read-out. The analysis reports the contribution of timescale heterogeneity with uncertainty, including when the increment does not differ from zero. The study that introduced \WESAD{} reports three-class chest accuracy of $76.5\%$ at macro-F1 $\approx 0.72$ with engineered window features and conventional classifiers under the same leave-one-subject-out protocol, and binary stress-detection accuracy of $\approx 93\%$ \cite{Schmidt2018WESAD}. The device-reservoir scores below are of the same order. In the wider literature (\cref{tab:ch5_sota}), differences in metrics and validation protocols preclude a direct ranking.

\paragraph{Demonstration~A: a single composition suffices for a single-timescale feature.} Binary stress-versus-baseline classification from electrodermal activity, read out at the window level from the lead composition alone, reaches a leave-one-subject-out macro-F1 of $0.894$ (\cref{fig:ch5_wesad}a). This supports the Demonstration-A claim that one well-matched composition can act as a useful affect-aligned node. It comes, however, with an instructive caveat: a static feature baseline (the mean, standard deviation, last value and slope of the same signal) matches it ($0.899$). The 60-second-window task is therefore quasi-static (the discriminative information sits in the instantaneous signal level, not in its temporal history), which is exactly why timescale heterogeneity has nothing to contribute here, and why a memory-demanding reformulation is needed to test the heterogeneity claim fairly.

\paragraph{Demonstration~B: streaming three-class tracking.} The fair test is a streaming one: the reservoir runs continuously over each session and the affective class (baseline, stress, or amusement) is read out at \emph{every} step, so that the task is designed to use fading memory, not a static window summary. \Cref{tab:ch5_wesad} decomposes the result into four matched-size banks. For reference, trivial baselines scored under the identical leave-one-subject-out protocol sit far below: a majority-class predictor reaches macro-F1 $0.23$ and a stratified-random predictor $0.33$, so the reservoir's $0.758$ is well above chance and not an artefact of class imbalance. Significance of the bank comparisons is assessed across the fifteen \emph{independent} held-out subjects, so the within-subject temporal autocorrelation of a per-step stream does not inflate the test. Relative to instantaneous input, adding dimensionality (a same-size memoryless bank) contributes $+0.028$, adding fading memory of a single timescale a further $+0.016$, and adding timescale heterogeneity a final $+0.005$. The measured fading-memory gain over a same-size memoryless bank is a modest but consistent $+0.014$ to $+0.020$. The timescale-heterogeneity increment, however, is $+0.005 \pm 0.011$, within seed noise (positive in seven of ten seeds; across sampling intervals from $0.5$ to \SI{4}{\second} the heterogeneous-minus-homogeneous gap stays in $[+0.002,+0.007]$ but never separates from zero). Per class, the best bank reaches F1 $0.90$ on baseline and $0.92$ on stress but only $0.55$ on the amusement class, which is the hard class for every model. The reason is an elicitation limit, not a memory-horizon one: amusement is the shortest \WESAD{} condition (roughly $5.6$\,ks of labelled data against $17.6$\,ks of baseline and $10.0$\,ks of stress) and the lowest in autonomic arousal, so its physiological signature is both weak and under-represented. Consistently, no bank from memoryless to heterogeneous recovers it, which is why the class limits every model instead of discriminating between them.

\paragraph{Why heterogeneity does not improve the label scores.} The affect labels are slowly varying sustained states, and their discriminative memory is dominated by the slow tonic band already covered by the lead composition ($\tau\approx19$--$26$\,s). A wider timescale distribution improves performance when a target requires several lags simultaneously, as in the memory-capacity and physiological-context tasks (\cref{fig:ch5_mc_curve,tab:ch5_physio}). The sustained label task does not show that requirement. Under this protocol, fading memory helps classification, but the additional heterogeneity increment is not resolved.

\section{Continuous Affect: Valence and Arousal}\label{sec:ch5_case}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The \WESAD{} tasks of \cref{sec:ch5_wesad} are \emph{categorical} and, as their static-feature tie showed, dominated by sustained states. The complementary and more demanding affective target is \emph{dimensional}: the continuous valence (pleasantness) and arousal (activation) a person feels moment to moment, which carry a fast phasic component and a slow tonic drift at once and so are intrinsically multi-timescale~\cite{Picard1997}. This is the target on which a \emph{spread} of timescales should matter where a single one cannot, and which the sustained \WESAD{} labels could not expose. It is tested here on a third independent corpus.

The Continuously Annotated Signals of Emotion corpus (\CASE{}) of Sharma et al.~\cite{Sharma2019CASE} records thirty subjects who rated valence and arousal continuously on a joystick (range $[0.5,9.5]$, \SI{20}{\hertz}) while watching emotional videos, with synchronised physiology at \SI{1}{\kilo\hertz}. The reservoir is run over the eight emotional videos at $\Delta t=\SI{1}{\second}$ on the same slow channel set as \WESAD{} (electrodermal activity, respiration, skin temperature, and an electrocardiogram-derived heart rate), per-subject robustly scaled, with the state reset and a short washout dropped at each video boundary so no memory bleeds across stimuli; a single linear ridge read-out then predicts valence and arousal, leave-one-subject-out, scored by the concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), the standard continuous-affect metric~\cite{Lin1989CCC}.

Every fading-memory bank outperforms the instantaneous read on the \CASE{} task (the heterogeneous bank by $+0.079$ mean CCC, positive in $24$ of $30$ subjects, paired Wilcoxon $p<10^{-4}$). A multi-$\tau$ filter bank raises CCC from $0.23$ for a single timescale to $0.41$ ($+0.176$, $27/30$ subjects, $p<10^{-6}$). Timescale spread therefore improves this continuously annotated target, consistent with the memory-capacity and physiological-context results of \cref{sec:ch5_benchmarks,sec:ch5_physio} and unlike the sustained \WESAD{} labels.

On this smooth regression, a linear multi-$\tau$ filter uses the timescale spread more effectively than the device model. A per-channel device bank reaches CCC $0.358$, compared with $0.413$ for the linear bank ($-0.047$, $p<10^{-2}$); random cross-channel mixing in the standard bank reduces the score by a further $\approx0.014$. The device models therefore do not outperform an equivalent digital filter on this task. Their proposed advantage is passive material-level filtering at the core-power estimate of \cref{sec:ch5_deploy}. The increment from device-internal heterogeneity is within seed noise ($+0.005$, $p=0.18$), because the jittered homogeneous bank already contains a timescale spread (\siTempRef{app:ch5_case}).

(a)~Valence and (b)~arousal. \section{Continuous Monitoring Under Deployment Conditions}\label{sec:ch5_robust}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The scores of \cref{sec:ch5_wesad} are dominated by long stretches of sustained state, where the instantaneous signal level already carries the label, which is precisely why a static feature baseline ties the reservoir on the quasi-static window task. A wearable in the field is judged on what those averages hide. It must keep working while the wearer moves and the sensor is noisy, and motion artefact is the dominant failure mode of wrist-worn electrodermal and photoplethysmographic sensing. This is a temporal problem on which fading memory has a structural advantage: a leaky integrator is a temporal low-pass that averages transient artefact away, whereas an instantaneous read reacts to every spike.

\Cref{fig:ch5_robust_monitoring} tests a continuous binary stress detector (stress versus baseline plus amusement, read out at every step, leave-one-subject-out, with the same linear read-out used throughout). On clean streams, all banks reach macro-F1 $\approx0.91$--$0.92$, comparable to the $\approx93\%$ binary benchmark for the corpus~\cite{Schmidt2018WESAD}. With increasing sensor noise and motion-artefact bursts, the instantaneous and memoryless controls fall from $0.914$ to $0.840$ at $\sigma=0.4$ of the signal range, while the fading-memory banks remain near $0.92$. At $\sigma=0.4$, the heterogeneous bank exceeds the instantaneous read by $+0.088$ for all fifteen subjects (paired Wilcoxon $p<10^{-4}$). On clean streams, fading memory reduces the false-alarm rate from $0.046$ to $0.026$. Homogeneous and heterogeneous banks are equally robust, so memory rather than its spread accounts for this result. Near label transitions, the increase from $0.747$ to $0.775$ is not resolved across subjects, and detection latency is similar across banks because it is set by the prediction-smoothing horizon.

\paragraph{Real motion artefact, not a Gaussian proxy.} The corruption above is synthetic, so the test was repeated with the noise \emph{gated on the measured chest accelerometer}: at each step the injected amplitude tracks the wearer's actual movement, concentrating artefact where the body moves instead of spreading it uniformly. The ordering replicates: the instantaneous read falls (macro-F1 $0.914\to0.898$ at the strongest gating) while the memory bank holds near $0.92$, a $+0.032$ advantage that is positive across subjects (paired $p=0.04$), and a classical moving average again matches the device ($+0.036$; \siTempRef{app:ch5_robust}). The effect is smaller than under the aggressive synthetic injection because the chest strap is mechanically stable and the protocol largely seated (the wrist, where motion artefact is severe, is the harder site examined in \cref{sec:ch5_deploy}), but the robustness conclusion does not rest on the Gaussian noise model.

\paragraph{Classical-filter control.} An exponential moving average applied to the same raw channels tests whether the noise robustness requires the device model. A single time constant matched to the lead cell reaches macro-F1 $0.927$ at $\sigma=0.4$, compared with $0.840$ for the instantaneous read and $0.916$ for the device bank ($+0.091$ over instantaneous, all fifteen subjects, $p<10^{-4}$). Spreading the moving-average timescales adds $+0.005$ (\textit{ns}), and the device's compressive nonlinearity adds $-0.008$ relative to that linear filter bank (\textit{ns}; \siTempRef{tab:ch5_filter}). The robustness is therefore a property of fading-memory filtering rather than a device-specific nonlinearity. The proposed device contribution is to perform this filtering through material relaxation, with no trained dynamics and at the core-power estimate of \cref{sec:ch5_deploy}. The multi-timescale benchmarks of \cref{sec:ch5_benchmarks,sec:ch5_physio} distinguish the heterogeneous bank; this single-timescale stress detector does not.

\paragraph{Cross-corpus replication.} A single corpus cannot establish generality, so the robustness test was repeated on an independent dataset: the PhysioNet Non-EEG corpus of Birjandtalab et al.~\cite{Birjandtalab2016}, twenty subjects wearing a \emph{different} wrist sensor (an Affectiva Q recording electrodermal activity and skin temperature, with a pulse-oximeter heart rate) through relaxation and cognitive, emotional, and physical stress phases. Taking cognitive and emotional stress as the stress class against relaxation (the psychosocial analogue of the \WESAD{} stressor, with the physical-exertion phase held out as a different mechanism whose racing heart rate is trivially separable from the instantaneous signal alone) and running the identical detector reproduces the \WESAD{} result on this independent sensor and population (\cref{tab:ch5_crosscorpus}). The corpus is harder in absolute terms (clean macro-F1 $\approx 0.65$--$0.68$, against $\approx 0.91$ on the cleaner \WESAD{} chest signals) and, as on \WESAD{}, the clean-stream memory advantage is small and not resolved. But under sensor noise the same divergence reappears, more sharply: the instantaneous detector falls from $0.651$ to $0.516$ while the fading-memory bank holds near $0.66$, a $+0.130$ advantage at $\sigma=0.4$ that is positive in nineteen of the twenty subjects (paired Wilcoxon $p<10^{-5}$). The noise-robustness of the temporal substrate is therefore not a property of one dataset; it transfers to a new corpus, sensor, and population.

\section{Toward a Worn Device: Sensor Site and Operating Budget}\label{sec:ch5_deploy}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The robustness of the previous section is a laboratory statement until two further questions are answered: whether the advantage survives a consumer-grade sensor, and what the device would cost to run. Both are answered from the same measured ingredients (the \WESAD{} wrist recordings and the device constants of \cref{ch:poc}) without any new assumption.

\paragraph{Sensor site.} \WESAD{} records each session with both a chest strap and an Empatica E4 wrist band. The wrist band has fewer and noisier channels: skin conductance, skin temperature, and photoplethysmography-derived heart rate, without respiration. On the wrist data (\cref{tab:ch5_wrist}), the clean macro-F1 of the instantaneous detector falls from $0.914$ to $0.881$, whereas the fading-memory reservoir changes from $0.920$ to $0.922$. The memory advantage is consequently larger on the wrist than on the chest ($+0.041$ versus $+0.006$ on clean streams and $+0.069$ under injected noise). Fading memory also reduces the wrist false-alarm rate from $0.062$ to $0.040$.

\paragraph{Operating budget.} The device dynamics are fixed, and only a linear read-out is fitted by one closed-form ridge solve. The trained component is an $(N{+}1)\times K$ weight matrix: $75$ weights for the $N=24$, three-class banks and $147$ for the $N=48$ banks. The deep models commonly used for wearable affect contain $10^{4}$--$10^{6}$ trainable weights, two to four orders of magnitude more. Using the measured proof-of-concept energy of $\approx\SI{50}{\nano\joule}$ per write event ($\approx\SI{6}{\femto\joule\per\micro\metre\squared}$ over a \SI{0.0825}{\square\centi\metre} junction), charging $N=24$ nodes once per second corresponds to $\approx\SI{1.2}{\micro\watt}$. The linear read-out requires $N\,K$ multiply--accumulates, well under a nanojoule per step. These estimates use unoptimised large-area devices; smaller junctions would reduce the event energy.

The estimate covers only the reservoir core: device charging and read-out operations. It excludes the sensor analogue front-end, analogue-to-digital conversion, and microcontroller overhead and is not a full system-power budget. The device response remains faster than the sub-hertz physiological signals considered here, and its relaxation performs the temporal filtering during the write/read cycle. The assumptions are tabulated in \siTempRef{app:ch5_deploy}.

\section{Matching Device and Task Timescales}\label{sec:ch5_designrules}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Performance depends on the overlap between device and task timescales (\cref{fig:ch5_tau_coverage}). In the single-composition sweep, the lead cell gives the highest memory capacity for the seconds-scale benchmark (\cref{fig:ch5_composition_sweep}). For the multi-lag physiological task, no single cell covers the full delay range, and the heterogeneous bank gives the highest single-bank score (\cref{tab:ch5_physio}).

\paragraph{Effect of timescale breadth.} \Cref{tab:ch5_summary} separates the effect of fading memory from that of timescale breadth. Both homogeneous and heterogeneous memory banks outperform memoryless and instantaneous baselines, with gains of up to $+0.08$ macro-F1 under sensor noise (\cref{sec:ch5_robust,sec:ch5_deploy}). Heterogeneity provides an additional gain when several lags are required: memory capacity increases by a factor of $1.54$ ($p=0.002$; \cref{fig:ch5_mc_curve}), and physiological-context reconstruction improves by $+0.012$ held-out $R^2$ over the best homogeneous bank ($15/15$ subjects, $p<10^{-4}$; \cref{tab:ch5_physio}). A multi-$\tau$ filter similarly improves continuous valence/arousal tracking by $+0.176$ CCC ($27/30$ subjects, $p<10^{-6}$; \cref{sec:ch5_case}). When one slow timescale dominates, as in the sustained stress labels and single-channel monitoring task, the best-matched homogeneous bank is as good as or marginally better than the heterogeneous bank. A composition-diverse bank is therefore useful for an unknown mixture of tasks: it loses at most about $0.01$ macro-F1 on the single-timescale tasks considered here and gains more on the multi-timescale tasks. The available variation comes from composition and device-to-device scatter; chemistry and drive amplitude provide additional illustrative axes in \cref{ch:comparative}.

\paragraph{Operating point and integration.} The models apply in the measured sub-kilohertz regime, which covers the physiological signals used here but not fast logic. \section{Limitations and Scope Conditions}\label{sec:ch5_limitations}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The results are subject to the following scope conditions.

\paragraph{In silico.} The demonstrations are simulations. Each node is the measured $\phi \otimes \lambda \otimes f$ behavioural model of \cref{sec:ch5_model}, not a bench reservoir, and every figure caption names the data behind it; the step from a fitted per-device model to a wired hardware reservoir is left to future work.

\paragraph{The composition assumption.} As detailed in \cref{sec:ch5_model}, write nonlinearity and fading memory were measured separately, at different write amplitudes and one inter-pulse cadence. Combining them assumes that the two regimes are compatible and probably underestimates the coupling between drive and retention. In a sensitivity analysis, each node's retention was rescaled with its write strength across a wide coupling range. The heterogeneous-to-homogeneous capacity ratio remained between $1.15$ and $1.53$ (\siTempRef{app:ch5_controls}). The gold/\TMPE{} negative-polarity measurements provide one empirical coupling estimate: write depth and forgetting time have a rank correlation of $\rho=+0.46$ across $28$ single-run traces. Calibrating the sensitivity model to this value gives $\kappa^{\star}=+0.18$, at which the capacity ratio is $1.45$ ($p=2\times10^{-3}$; \siTempRef{app:ch5_controls}). That corpus also shows interval-dependent potentiation, but its host, electrode, and polarity differ from the lead-cell model. A matched-amplitude experiment on the lead composition that varies pulse count and inter-pulse interval together would remove this assumption.

\paragraph{Operating regime and read-out.} The model is identified only in the measured sub-kilohertz regime; fast temporal tasks are outside its scope. Every read-out is a single ridge regression because a nonlinear read-out could supply the task nonlinearity independently of the reservoir. The read is assumed to be sub-threshold and state-preserving.

\paragraph{Heterogeneity axes.} The quantitative heterogeneous banks span the replicated \PEO{}/\LiTf{} composition grid. The chemistry and drive-amplitude axes of \cref{ch:comparative}, being sample-limited ($n\le 2$ per matched cell), enter only as illustrative additional controls; Demonstration~B is therefore a principle demonstration of composition heterogeneity, not a powered comparison across chemistries. The composition cells that define these banks index \emph{composition}, not film thickness: although spin-coating speed and residual film thickness covary with the \PEO{} fraction, the parameter cards inherited here were shown to be governed by composition, not thickness (\cref{fig:ch4_thickness_control}, \cref{sec:ch4_materials}), so the heterogeneity exploited in this chapter is compositional in origin.

\paragraph{Corpora and negative controls.} The affective results use \WESAD{} as the main corpus. Noise robustness is replicated on the PhysioNet Non-EEG dataset (\cref{sec:ch5_robust}), and continuous valence and arousal are tested on \CASE{} (\cref{sec:ch5_case}). A broader multi-corpus study remains future work. Two comparisons do not show a device-specific improvement: timescale heterogeneity does not separately improve final-label classification when one slow timescale dominates (\cref{sec:ch5_wesad}), and the nonlinear device encoding does not exceed a linear multi-$\tau$ filter on smooth continuous-affect regression (\cref{sec:ch5_case}). By contrast, the heterogeneous bank improves the multi-timescale memory-capacity and physiological-context tasks.

\section{Summary and Bridge to the Conclusions}\label{sec:ch5_conclusions}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Behavioural models fitted to the measurements of \cref{ch:comparative} support temporal-computing simulations with a linear read-out. A heterogeneous bank raises memory capacity by a factor of $1.54$ and improves multi-lag physiological-context reconstruction by $+0.012$ held-out $R^2$ over the best homogeneous bank. On \WESAD{}, the models reach leave-one-subject-out macro-F1 scores of $0.894$ for binary stress and $0.758$ for three-class streaming classification. Under injected sensor noise, fading-memory banks remain near $0.92$ macro-F1 while the instantaneous baseline falls to $0.84$ ($+0.088$, all fifteen subjects, $p<10^{-4}$). A classical moving average matches this robustness, so it is attributable to fading-memory filtering rather than the model's nonlinearity. The proposed material implementation would realise that filter at an estimated reservoir-core power of about a microwatt, with tens to hundreds of trained linear-read-out weights.

The additional value of a timescale distribution depends on the task. It is resolved for random-input memory, physiological-context reconstruction, and continuous valence/arousal tracking, all of which depend on several lags. It is not resolved for the sustained \WESAD{} labels or single-timescale stress monitoring. Composition-dependent relaxation and within-composition scatter provide the distributions used in these simulations.

The next experimental steps are a matched-amplitude pulse protocol that varies pulse count and cadence, a replicated study of chemistry and drive-amplitude heterogeneity, and a wired device bank that tests the behavioural-model predictions in hardware.
